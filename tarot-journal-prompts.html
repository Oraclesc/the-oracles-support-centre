Tarot Journal Prompts

A Tarot journal can turn occasional readings into a genuine learning record. Use these prompts to study the cards, examine your interpretations and connect symbolic reflection with real-world observation.

1

Daily Card

Draw one card and write: What did I notice first? What is happening in the image? What traditional meaning fits? What might this theme look like in ordinary life?

2

Describe Before Interpreting

Choose a card and describe only what you can see for two minutes. Record people, objects, direction of movement, setting, posture, weather, symbols and visual contrasts. Interpret only after the description is complete.

3

First Impression vs Guidebook

Write your own interpretation before checking a traditional meaning. Then compare the two. What did you notice that the guide emphasised? What did you interpret differently?

4

Card of the Week

Keep the same card visible for a week. At the end of each day, record where its themes did or did not appear. This is an observation exercise, not a search for proof that the card predicted events.

5

Major Arcana Study

Choose one Major Arcana card. Record its key imagery, number, traditional themes, questions it raises and one way its meaning could change depending on the spread position.

6

Minor Arcana Study

Choose one suit and compare its Ace through Ten. Write one sentence about the developing story of the number sequence, then note where the individual cards break the pattern.

7

Court Card Practice

Choose a Page, Knight, Queen or King. Describe it first as a person, then as a quality, then as an approach to a situation. Which interpretation best fits your question?

8

Reversal Practice

Take an upright card and explore three possible reversal approaches: blocked or delayed energy, an internal/private expression, and an altered or excessive expression. Decide which approach fits the context.

9

Three-Card Reading Review

Record the exact question, the three positions, your first impression and your final interpretation. A week later, revisit the entry and note what you understand differently.

10

Relationship Reflection

What do I need from this connection? What am I assuming rather than knowing? What communication needs attention? What boundary would support my wellbeing? What action is actually within my control?

11

Career Reflection

What opportunity am I considering? What practical facts do I already know? What information is missing? What strength could I use? What is one real-world action I can take next?

12

Decision-Making Check

Write the decision without the cards first. Then use a spread to generate questions or perspectives. Finally, write your decision again using the evidence available to you. Notice whether the cards clarified your thinking or simply reflected your uncertainty.

13

Symbolism Journal

Pick five symbols from one card. For each, record what it might traditionally represent, what it means to you personally and what evidence in the image supports your interpretation.

14

Card Combination

Draw two cards. Find one shared theme, one contrast and one way the second card could change the emphasis of the first. Avoid simply adding two lists of keywords together.

15

Reading Bias Check

Before a reading, write down what outcome you hope for and what outcome you fear. Afterward, check whether those expectations influenced how you interpreted the cards.

16

Reassurance Check

If you feel an urge to repeat a reading, write down what you are hoping a new card will tell you. Consider whether a conversation, research or time would answer the underlying question more reliably.

17

Monthly Review

At the end of the month, look for repeated cards, suits, numbers and themes. Record patterns without assuming that repetition proves a prediction. Ask what the pattern teaches you about the questions you have been asking.

18

Learning Progress

Write five things you understand now that were confusing when you started. Then choose one skill to practise next month: imagery, numbers, suits, reversals, spreads, combinations or journaling.

19

Reading for Someone Else

Record what the person actually asked, what consent they gave and what boundaries were agreed. After the reading, note which statements were observations or possibilities rather than claims about private facts.

20

End-of-Reading Reflection

What was useful? What remained unclear? Did I make any claims stronger than the cards supported? What practical step, if any, will I take? What can I safely leave unresolved?

📝

A Simple Journal Template

Date:   Question:   Spread:   Cards:

First impression: What did I notice before checking a guide?

Interpretation: How do the cards relate to their positions and to one another?

Real-world context: What facts do I actually know?

Reflection: What question or action remains?

Later review: What changed, and what did I learn?
